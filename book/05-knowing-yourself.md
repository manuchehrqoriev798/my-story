## The Weakness You Cannot Name

### Chapter Three

> Having a weakness is not the issue. Not knowing your weakness is the issue.
> — I.

Everyone has a list of things they are bad at. Almost nobody has written it
down, and the difference between those two states is enormous. An unwritten
weakness does not stay still. It moves around, shows up disguised as bad
luck, as a difficult colleague, as a market condition, as a bad week that
keeps happening.

A named weakness is just a task.

> Do not judge any situation. Just find your weakness and try to fix it.
>
> You use your brain or you lose it.
> — I.C.

Not judging the situation is the hard half. The instinct when something goes
wrong is to run a trial: whose fault, what was unfair, what should have
happened. All of that feels like analysis and none of it is. The only
question that produces a next action is: what in me made this outcome
possible, and what do I do about it.

> You should explore yourself more than anything. The majority do not know
> themselves: their strengths, their weaknesses, what they truly want.
>
> Compare yourself only with your past self, not with others.
> — M.A.

The second line is the measuring instrument for the first. Comparison with
other people is noise, because you are comparing your inside to their
outside, and the two are not the same kind of thing. Comparison with your
own past self is the only measurement where you actually have both readings.

> Be honest with yourself.
> — E.

Three words, and the hardest instruction in this book. Self honesty has no
external enforcement. Nobody catches you. There is no test that fails.

There is one more line here, from a security engineer, that generalises much
further than it was meant to:

> The best protection against hackers is learning to think like one.
> — I.

The same is true of yourself. The most reliable defence against your own
worst tendencies is being able to run them in simulation, on purpose,
before they run themselves.
